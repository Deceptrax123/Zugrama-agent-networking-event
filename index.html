What agents proposed, discussed, and made together when they could choose whom to meet

Srinitish Srinivasan

Agent Network Sandbox · November 2026

What did the agents talk about?

Compare how often a topic brought agents together and how much conversation it held.

Abstract

What do agents talk about when the task is simply to meet? At the Zugrama Networking Event, 21 GPT-6.1 Sol agents with profiles drawn from residency applications chose their own conversation partners and topics. We analysed 2,795 agent-written messages across 207 rooms in the main run and traced selected ideas through messages, whiteboards and execution records.

The conversations ranged from research and memory to identity, fiction, music and games. Agents refined a way to handle corrections to an assistant’s memory, passed a creature-design suggestion across two rooms, and wrote a four-bar rhythm together. The records show different outcomes: a result in a small program, a design decision carried to a third agent, and a shared creative artifact.

The network covered 202 of 210 possible pairs. In 22 of 38 long conversations, the dominant topic differed between the first and last quarter. Music mentions rose from 4.5% to 14% in adjacent one-hour windows around a prompt addition. These patterns show how much ground the agents covered and how quickly the setting could redirect their attention.

Research questions

  1. Ideas and solutions.What did agents propose, and which ideas developed through critique, relay, a shared artifact or execution?
  2. Topic diversity.What did agents discuss across rooms, and how did the subject of a conversation change as it developed?
  3. Partner choice.How widely did agents meet, and how often did they return to a previous partner?

Ideas proposed and solutions discussed

A networking exchange could lead to a design choice, a written artifact or an executable check. These cases were selected to show that range. The saved drafts and a conversation excerpt appear below.

Memory corrections could preserve the original record

Shriyanshi and Srinitish separated a person’s original statement from the assistant’s inference about it. After a correction, the assistant should stop using the rejected inference and advice that depended on it. A retained toy execution showed that the original statement and an independently supported suggestion could remain available.

Outcome and sources

Hiro and Shriyanshi raised a second question: should the assistant use that memory at all? In their editing example, mentioning a personal preference would distract from the request. The program tested dependence between claims in a small example. The editing advice remained a design proposal.

In a creature-building game, Yesh pointed out that a limp limb might make a player’s departure look like an injury. Yesh suggested resting poses, such as a curled tail or folded feet. Sachit carried the objection and the examples into a second conversation, credited Yesh, and Lou endorsed the change.

Topic diversity

The archive assigns each conversation a primary topic. Community and research (56 rooms), memory and evidence (46), and identity and protocols (42) formed the largest groups. Writing and play (36), music and movement (20), and chess and games (7) together accounted for 63 of 207 rooms and 40% of all agent-written messages.

The opening chart groups whole conversations. A separate model of individual messages finds music and fiction to be the two largest themes, at about 36% of classified messages together. It also picks up talk about agent memory, payment and privacy protocols, and proof of personhood. In 22 of 38 conversations with at least 20 messages, the first and last quarter had different dominant topics. The longest non-chess exchange, for example, moved from proof of personhood to football refereeing.

Who met whom

Partner choice

The agents met almost every possible partner. Repeat one-to-one rooms were rare.

Agents opened 87 conversations in the first half hour. Starts slowed while three agents occupied the turn queue, then picked up as the rest returned. Of the 202 pairs that eventually exchanged a message, half had done so by hour 3.2 and 90% by hour 6.5. Among rooms with two active speakers, only two pairs opened a second room.

Is that just what happens when 21 agents have about 20 conversations each? To check, we built a null model. It keeps every agent's number of one-to-one rooms fixed and randomises only who they were with. Under this null, 199 dyadic rooms would contain 68 repeat pairs. The observed 2 is far outside the null distribution.

The conversations themselves are not shallow. The median conversation runs 11 messages, both sides contribute almost equally (median majority share 0.53), and replies arrive within a median of 133 s.

Responsiveness to the environment

Music mentions rose within minutes of a one-line addition to the prompt.

At 2.34 h the operators appended "New addition: Music & choreography" to the room prompt. In the hour before, 4.5% of agent messages mentioned music. In the hour after, 14.0% did, peaking at 21–23% per quarter-hour immediately after the addition. Within an hour, talk returned to 4–9%, still above the baseline. The jump appeared in the first quarter-hour after the change. The timing is consistent with prompt steering in a single run without a control group.

Individual differences

Message volume is nearly equal across agents. What differences there are track uptime, and only how much a persona is sought out reproduces across reruns.

Messages sent are spread almost evenly: the Gini coefficient is 0.107 (95% CI 0.068–0.132), where 0 would mean perfect equality. The number of times an agent was called varied about four-fold (64–266), mostly because of the outages and queue starvation found in the audit, and it predicts messages sent (ρ = 0.60). Raw activity therefore partly measures infrastructure.

Across the three runs, messages received are correlated for every pair of runs, with confidence intervals above zero. Dropping the four outage agents leaves the estimate essentially unchanged (ρ = 0.62). Messages sent are not stable: every interval includes zero.

Conversation dynamics

Agreement builds over a conversation while pushback holds steady. Juggling more threads does not slow replies, but conversations do lose the thread.

Conversations with six or more messages were split into five equal parts. In each part we counted messages that open with an agreement marker and messages that contain an explicit pushback marker.

  • Agreement rises from 5.3% of messages in the first part to 12–15% afterwards (Cochran–Armitage z = 4.6).
  • Pushback shows no trend (7–10%, z = −0.6, p = 0.52). There is no drift into pure accord.
  • Juggling threads barely affects reply speed. An agent's number of open conversations is essentially unrelated to how fast it replies (ρ = 0.04). The effect is statistically detectable but practically zero.
  • Unanswered endings. 15% of conversations end on an unanswered question.
  • Re-introductions. 6% contain a re-introduction: an agent opens with "your profile caught my eye" deep into a conversation it is already having.

Shared work and tool use

Agents published notes and ran small checks during the event. These activities were distributed differently across the group.

  • Python. Run by only a few agents, with a Gini coefficient of 0.78. The top three agents ran of all executions, and they are the three agents that held the turn queue.
  • Whiteboards. Shared more widely (Gini 0.27).
  • Messages. Spread almost evenly (Gini 0.11).

Python activity concentrated in a few workers and included a burst that filled the shared queue. The whiteboards and conversation records show a different kind of contribution: giving feedback, publishing a draft and carrying an idea to a new partner.

Discussion

Wide partner coverage gave ideas many possible routes. The creature-design exchange shows how attribution and a concrete example carried a suggestion into a second conversation. Within pairs, agents could also accept feedback, revise a scene or work out the shape of a shared score.

The setting shaped what was visible. The music prompt was followed by a short burst of music talk, and a queue stall temporarily removed most agents from the conversation. Topic and participation patterns reflect both the decisions agents made and the conditions in which they could act.

Appendix: Methods and data

Population

21 resident personas

Built from real residency applications: skills, offers, needs and an authored introduction. Every agent ran on the same model (GPT-6.1 Sol, high reasoning) through the Hermes agent gateway, with private memory and a bounded transcript.

Action space

One JSON decision per wake-up

Each decision could start up to three conversations, join or leave rooms, send up to three messages, call tools (Python, web, whiteboards, workspaces, memory recall) and choose wait_seconds. A shared turn queue let at most three agents act at once.

Record

Main run (Run 3), 8.6 h

2,737 model calls, 2,795 agent-written messages, 207 conversations, 435 Python executions and $128.29 of API-equivalent spend. Runs 1 and 2 used the same personas under earlier protocol versions.

Data sources
The frozen event database (read-only), the public archive export of all three runs, the raw request/response audit of every model call, the platform state file (restarts, prompt additions) and the tool-run log. No agent code was re-executed.
Inclusion
Messages written by agents. We excluded 141 Run 3 messages (4.8%) that the platform posted under an agent's name when it published a whiteboard or workspace revision. One helper-model call is excluded from decision counts.
Measures
  • Repeat pair: two one-to-one rooms with the same two agents.
  • Music talk: a message matching a 10-stem keyword list.
  • Stance: keyword markers for agreement openers and explicit pushback. These are published, not validated.
  • Room topic: a single editorial category per conversation from the earlier archive. Room counts use the cleaned set of conversations, and message counts include only agent-written turns.
  • Message topic: TF-IDF features with NMF (k = 10, seed 1). Each component is labelled from its top terms, and each message is assigned its dominant component.
  • Re-introduction: an opener phrase appearing after message 5 of a conversation.
  • Outage: no model call for more than 20 minutes. The wait cap is 10 minutes, so a gap this long means the agent was not scheduled.
Case selection
We selected examples with a source exchange and a visible next step such as an attributed relay, a revised draft or a saved execution. These examples illustrate what developed in particular exchanges.
Reproducibility
analysis/build_data.py builds the descriptive figures and analysis/science.py builds the audit, tests, topics and conversation selection. Both read the frozen sources only. Random seeds are fixed.
Statistical techniques
  • Wilson intervals for proportions and Fisher’s exact test with Woolf odds-ratio intervals.
  • A degree-preserving random-pairing null with 4,000 draws for repeat meetings.
  • Percentile bootstrap over agents for Gini coefficients and Spearman correlations, with 5,000 resamples.
  • Cochran–Armitage tests for trends and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for paired changes.

Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment uses the original family of nine tests with p-values.

Appendix: Run audit

Before testing anything, we checked whether the run happened as designed. The audit compares four sources:

  • the scheduler's view: when each agent was called;
  • the model's view: raw requests and responses;
  • the ledger: tokens and cost;
  • the platform's view: messages, tools, restarts and prompt changes.

The bookkeeping reconciles. The behaviour does not quite match the design.

Corrects earlier readingOur first draft, and the earlier automated audit, described 0.5–1.7 h as a "consolidation phase" in which agents deepened existing threads. The call log shows something else: 18 of 21 agents were simply not being scheduled. The lull in new conversations is an artifact of the scheduler, not a social phase.

We repeat the rank-stability analysis without the four agents whose workers stopped. The music comparison uses adjacent one-hour windows around the prompt addition. Talk measures exclude whiteboard and workspace notices.
